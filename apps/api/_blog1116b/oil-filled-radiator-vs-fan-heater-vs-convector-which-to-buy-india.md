# Oil-Filled Radiator vs Fan Heater vs Convector: Which Should You Buy in India?

**Short answer:** Buy an **oil-filled radiator** for a bedroom or study you sit in for hours, especially at night. It is silent, gives steady heat and does not blow dry air at you. Buy a **fan heater** (PTC or ceramic) for quick, short bursts of warmth: ten minutes before a bath, a cold morning at a desk, or carrying it from room to room. A **convector** sits in between. It is cheaper than a radiator and quieter than a fan heater, but it heats more slowly than a fan heater and loses heat as soon as it switches off. None of the three is "free" to run. Your bill depends on wattage, hours and your tariff, not on the heater type.

Pick the *type* first: a cheap heater of the wrong type wastes more money than a pricier one of the right type. If you already know you want a radiant heater (halogen, quartz or carbon), read our [quartz vs halogen vs carbon heater comparison](/blog/quartz-vs-halogen-vs-carbon-heater-difference-india) instead.

## How each heater type works

**Oil-filled radiator (OFR).** An electric element sits inside sealed metal fins filled with oil. The element heats the oil, the oil heats the fins, and the fins warm the air around them, which rises and circulates slowly through the room. The oil is a sealed heat store, never topped up. Because the fins take time to warm up, the room warms gradually. When the thermostat cuts power, the hot fins keep releasing heat for a while.

**Fan heater (PTC / ceramic / coil).** A heating element (a ceramic PTC plate in most current models, a bare coil in older cheap ones) sits behind a small fan that pushes hot air out. You feel warmth within a minute. The trade-off is noise from the fan, moving air that can feel dry, and quick cooling once it switches off.

**Convector heater.** A flat panel or box with an element at the bottom. Cold air enters low, warms over the element and leaves through the top grille. There is no oil and usually no fan (some add a "turbo" fan). They are light, often wall-mountable, and spread heat more evenly than a fan heater, but store no heat.

## Which one suits your room

**Bedroom used all night.** Choose an oil-filled radiator with a thermostat. Set it to a low step, let it reach temperature, and the thermostat will cycle it on and off. If dry throat or skin bothers you in winter, pair it with a humidifier. Our [cool mist vs warm mist humidifier guide](/blog/humidifier-buying-guide-india-cool-mist-vs-warm-mist) explains which type suits winter.

**Small study or work desk.** A fan heater wins. You only need to warm the space around you, not the whole room, and it does that in a minute. A compact ceramic model with a tip-over switch is enough.

**Large living room or hall.** An OFR with more fins and higher wattage, or a 2,000 W convector, spreads heat better than a fan heater, which only warms the area in front of it. Any heater struggles with high ceilings or open doors.

**Bathroom.** Only a heater the maker rates for bathroom use, mounted away from water.

**Homes with kids, pets or elderly people.** OFR fins get hot but not as hot as an exposed coil or glowing tube, and there is no open element, so it is the calmer choice. Still keep a gap and supervise. Radiant halogen or quartz heaters are the riskiest type here.

## Running cost: do the maths yourself

Every electric heater turns almost all the power it draws into heat. A 2,000 W radiator and a 2,000 W fan heater use the same electricity per hour **at full power**. The difference is in how they behave over a long session:

- **Units used = wattage (kW) × hours on full power.** A 2,000 W heater at full power for 1 hour uses 2 units (kWh). An 800 W setting for 1 hour uses 0.8 units.
- **Bill = units × your tariff per unit.** Check your electricity bill for your slab rate, since it differs by state and by slab.
- **Thermostat cycling cuts real use.** Once the room reaches the set temperature, a thermostat switches the element off and on. A heater that runs, say, 60% of the hour uses roughly 60% of its rated units. An OFR's stored heat helps it ride through the off periods, which is why it suits long sessions.
- **Fan heaters are cheaper for short runs.** If you only need 15-20 minutes of heat, a fan heater's quick warm-up means you switch it off sooner.

## Safety checklist for any type

1. **Tip-over switch.** It cuts power if the heater falls over. This is essential with kids or pets.
2. **Overheat protection.** It switches off if internal temperature climbs too high.
3. **Thermostat**, not just power steps. It saves units and avoids overheating the room.
4. **Plug straight into a wall socket rated for the load.** Do not use a thin extension board for a 2,000 W heater. It can overheat.
5. **Keep about 1 metre clear** of curtains, bedding, clothes and furniture.
6. **Ventilation and dry air.** Electric heaters do not burn oxygen, but a closed room can feel stuffy and dry. Our guide on [whether room heaters reduce oxygen](/blog/does-room-heater-reduce-oxygen-dry-air-safety-tips-india) covers what is myth and what is real.
7. **Switch off before you leave** the room or the house. Prefer a radiator with a timer if you want heat through the night.

## Buying checklist

- **Fins (OFR):** More fins usually means more surface area and a higher wattage. Read the wattage on the label, not just the fin count. Two 11-fin heaters can draw very different power.
- **Power steps:** Look for 2-3 settings so you are not forced to run full power in a small room.
- **Castor wheels and handle (OFR):** Radiators are heavy, so you will move them more than you think.
- **PTC ceramic over bare coil (fan heater):** PTC elements self-limit their temperature. Bare coils run hotter and smell of burning dust.
- **Wall-mount option (convector):** It saves floor space and keeps it away from kids.
- **ISI mark and warranty:** Check the listing for both before you buy.

## FAQ

**Is an oil-filled radiator better than a fan heater?**
For long use in one room, yes. It is silent, the heat is steadier and the air feels less dry. For quick heat in a small space, a fan heater is better and cheaper to buy.

**Does an oil-filled heater consume less electricity?**
Not per hour at the same wattage. A 2,000 W radiator and a 2,000 W fan heater both use 2 units an hour at full power. A radiator with a thermostat can use less over a long session, because its stored heat lets the element stay off for longer stretches.

**Is it safe to sleep with an oil-filled radiator on?**
It is the safest of the common types for overnight use, provided it has a thermostat, tip-over and overheat protection, sits away from bedding and curtains, and is plugged straight into a wall socket. A timer is better still.

**Is a convector heater the same as an oil-filled radiator?**
No. Both warm the room by convection, but a convector heats air directly over an element and cools quickly when switched off. An oil-filled radiator heats sealed oil that keeps radiating warmth after the power cycles off.
